# Method: Intake, Comps, Range, Pricing Stance and Options

## Intake Checklist

Ask for what's missing in one message; use tappable choices for occupancy, timeline and pool when the runtime offers them.

With the home's MLS property report (`listing-sheet.md`), items 2, 3 (except CDD details it leaves blank), 5 and 12 are answered from the county record: confirm them in one line instead of asking. Items 4, 6, 7, 8 and 9 still come from the seller, and item 4 is "what changed since" the report's listing date.

**From the seller (about the home)**
1. Address, with the city and county: they set the closing costs, the taxes and the MLS. When only a street address is given, take them from stats.py's `subject_location` (the export's own row for the home) when it has them, and say so in the reply; otherwise ask. Never infer them from subdivision names or the MLS's area.
2. Beds, full and half baths, heated square feet, lot size, year built, construction (block or frame).
3. Pool (private or none), garage spaces, HOA and CDD (amounts, or none). An HOA adds the association's documents fee to the net sheet (an estoppel letter in Florida, a resale certificate in Texas; the net sheet says "HOA Documents" outside Florida). A condo also gets the association questions in `condo.md` (milestone inspection and SIRS status, special assessments, reserves, lender approval).
4. Updates with approximate dates: roof, AC, water heater, kitchen, baths, flooring, windows, electrical, plumbing, pool surface and equipment, and whether permits were pulled. Documented updates are worth money; claims aren't.
5. The current annual tax bill.
6. Known issues or past insurance claims. Most states require sellers to disclose known material defects, and they affect the price.
7. Flood history: any flood damage while they've owned it, flood insurance claims (including NFIP), and flood assistance (including FEMA). Where the market has `flood.seller_disclosure` (Florida: s. 689.302), the seller signs that disclosure at or before the contract, so collect the answers now and list the form under "What We Need from You".
8. Timeline to close, and whether the home will be occupied or vacant for showings.
9. Mortgage payoff (optional): ask for the lender's payoff statement (`mortgage_payoff`). A payoff the seller states ("about $171,500, from their September statement") is a payoff: `mortgage_payoff`, used as given. Only a loan balance understates it: give a balance as `mortgage_balance` with `mortgage_rate` and compute.py adds a month's interest (at that rate, else 4.5%), and the notes say it's an estimate. A figure the seller or agent gives from memory ("about $210,000") goes in `mortgage_payoff` too: the notes say it's the seller's estimate, and the reply asks for the payoff statement. A vaguer one ("maybe around $200k") stays out of the numbers; ask for the statement. With any of them, the net sheet ends in estimated cash at closing.

**From the agent**
10. The MLS CMA export (CSV) of nearby homes of the types the agent wants compared (single-family, and townhouses when they overlap): sales from about the last 6 months, plus active, pending, expired and canceled listings. For Stellar the columns are built in; another MLS needs its headers mapped (`--columns` and `export_columns`).
11. Brokerage terms (listing fee and buyer's agent compensation), if the agent gives them. Without them 5% total is assumed (2.5% each) and marked on every net; the agent can correct it after the first report.
12. Flood zone, if known. Otherwise write "to confirm". Never tell the seller that buyers won't need flood insurance: lenders require it in zones A and V, and in Florida Citizens requires it on many policies outside them (see the market's `flood` notes).

If the seller knows only some dates, go ahead and list the rest under "What We Need from You". Never guess a roof date, a permit, or a tax amount.

## Comps

For a condo, choose and adjust comps by `condo.md` instead of the rules below.

From `stats.py`'s `sold_candidates`, choose the closest 5 sales when 5 or more qualify (4 or 6 only with a reason in `method_note`, such as a sixth sale in the subject's own subdivision; 3 only when no more qualify), taking them in the ranking's order so the same export gives the same comps and the same median: same subdivision first, then a comparable neighborhood within about a mile; within about 20% of the size, same pool status, similar age and construction, closed within about 6 months. Include the sales that argue for a lower price; the seller's next agent will show them anyway.

Each candidate carries `flags`: `distressed` (REO, short sale, auction) and `new_construction`. Leave those out unless the market is mostly distressed or new construction (or the subject is), then adjust for it and explain why in `method_note`. The export holds the property types the agent chose to compare. Single-family homes and townhouses can be compared when they overlap in size and price, so neither is dropped: the ranking puts the subject's type first, then close types, and puts condos, 55+ communities, leased land, a different waterfront status or a different number of stories lower. `sold_by_type` shows the mix; when the comps span types, say so in `method_note`. Duplicate sale records are already dropped (see `market_notes`), and a relisted home shows once in the competition with `listings` and `earlier_prices`. The ranking already favors recent, close sales; `more_candidates` counts the ones not listed (re-run stats.py with `--limit 30` to see them). Pass `--as-of` with the date the export was pulled so months of supply runs to that day.

Give each comp a condition level from the condition ladder (`condition-ladder.md`), from its remarks, and the home its level from the seller's described updates (never from an old listing of the subject, including the remarks and photos in its property report); compute.py adjusts each comp by the difference between the levels, and a condition amount typed on a card stops it. Say that the condition levels are judgment calls based on listing text.

## Adjustments

Default rates come from the built-in market (`cma.adjustments`; built in for Florida: about $75/sq ft for differences under about 300 sq ft, $25,000 for a private pool, condition by the condition ladder's levels (`condition-ladder.md`: $15,000 for an updated kitchen, $45,000 for a full renovation, over an original home; the script takes the difference), the roof by its age on the as-of date (shingle: under 10 years $0, 10–14 years –$5,000, 15–19 years –$10,000, 20 or more –$15,000; double the years for tile or metal; adjust by the difference between the bands, so a comp with a 7-year roof against the subject's 16-year roof is –$10,000, and a comp in the subject's band gets nothing), –$5,000 when a comp has other documented recent systems (AC, water heater) that the seller's home can't match (never for the roof as well); the seller's own recent updates earn no credit until they're documented, –$5,000 to –$10,000 for a noticeably better lot or water, 1–2% per quarter when the market has softened and 0 for sales in the last 6 weeks or so). The time adjustment is the script's: state the rate once in `comps.time_adjustment` (`{"rate_per_quarter": 0.015}`, plus `"prices": "rising"` in a rising market and `"cutoff"` when it isn't the market split) and leave the time lines off the cards. compute.py gives every comp that closed before the cutoff the rate times the quarters from its close date to the as-of date, on its price net of seller-paid costs, to $100, and nothing to a sale on or after the cutoff; the close date comes from the export (or the card's `close_date`). A time amount typed on a card that disagrees stops the render; the card shows the script's own time line, so its bullets say why in words. Outside the built-in market, derive the rates from paired sales in the export, scaled to the price, and say so. Explain any departure in `method_note`.

The built-in rates are flat dollars from Central Florida sales in one price band (`cma.calibrated_for`). compute.py warns when the home is outside that area or band: then derive the rates from paired sales in the export, or use the agent's, and scale flat amounts (a pool, a renovation) to the price. There are no built-in rates for garage spaces, bedroom or bath count, age, view, or size differences over about 300 sq ft: derive those from paired sales and say so, or leave the difference to the range and explain it.

**No paired sales** (no export, or too few sales to pair): the rates are judgment. Say so in `method_note` and the reply, and keep them inside typical ranges, scaled to the price: size at about a quarter to a half of the area's price per square foot, a private pool about 3–6% of price, a full renovation vs. dated about 8–12%, full vs. partial about 5–7%. Make no market-time adjustment without sales that show a change; say the market was taken as flat. Keep the adjustments few and small, and let a wider range carry what you can't measure.

- Subtract seller-paid buyer costs from the sale price, dollar for dollar.
- List each comp's adjustments in the report data (`sold_price`, `seller_concessions`, `adjustments`); compute.py does the arithmetic and fills the card and the summary table from the same numbers. Adjusted values print to the nearest $100 (an odd seller credit gives $433,729, shown $433,700); the math keeps the exact figures. More than about 15% net or 25% gross of the sale price (common appraisal guidelines) means a weak comp: replace it, or explain why it stays.
- **Outliers.** After adjusting, a comp more than 10% above or below the median of the other comps (compute.py names it) is an outlier: replace it with the next candidate. Keep it only when it's one of the two closest matches in condition and location, say why in `method_note`, and don't let it set an end of the range. Decide once, before the range, so the same comps always give the same median.
- Write each adjustment as a sentence with its dollar amount, to the seller: "It sold earlier in the year, when rates were lower: minus about $10,000."

## The Range

- **Supported range:** the script's, by one rule, so the same comps always give the same range: the normal width (the widest $5,000 step under about 6% of the median adjusted value: $25,000 at $440,000, $20,000 at $386,800) centered on the median adjusted value, the low end rounded half up to $5,000 and the high end the low end plus the width, pulled in when the rounding would put an end past what the comps support (one comp never sets an end: an end stays at or inside the second-highest or second-lowest adjusted value, or half the normal width from the median, whichever reaches farther). compute.py prints it at the comps stage (report.json with the subject and the comps only) and uses it everywhere after: never type the range. The way to move it is the comps: replace an outlier or the weakest match (the largest adjustments, the farthest or oldest sale) and re-run. Say in `comps.lean` (judgment, no figures) which sales the evidence leans on.
- **The agent's own range:** only when the agent chose one themselves, give `range_override` `{low, high, reason}`: `reason` is judgment, figure-free, and the report shows the range as the agent's, beside the method's. compute.py still warns when an override is wider than the cap (`range_wide`), narrower than half of it (`range_narrow`) or set by one comp (`range_one_comp`), naming the method's range.
- **Appraisal ceiling:** name the highest similar sale. A contract well above it invites a low appraisal.

## Pricing Stance

You pick the stance; the script turns it into the list price and the options, by rule, so two runs on the same comps and the same stance list the same prices. Never type a list price.

| Stance (`pricing.stance`) | Where the list price sits | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| `draw_offers` | A quarter of the way up the range | Buyers have the upper hand: plenty of supply, many price cuts, sales under asking. A price buyers see as a bargain in the first weeks brings the most showings and, with luck, more than one offer |
| `market` | The middle of the range | A balanced market, or signals that point both ways |
| `premium` | Three quarters of the way up the range | Few homes for many buyers, sales at or over asking, and a home that stands out among the comps |

compute.py suggests one from the export (`stance.suggested`, with the signals behind it): **Draw Offers** when months of supply is 6 or more, 40% or more of the active listings have cut their price, or recent sales closed under 97% of their final asking price; **Premium** when supply is under 3 months and recent sales closed at or above their final asking price; else **Market Price**. A reprice or a relist never suggests Premium: the market already turned a price down. Without an export there's nothing to read, and it suggests Market Price. The comps stage prints the suggestion and each stance's list price here (`stance.prices`).

Keep the suggestion unless something about this home or this seller says otherwise (its condition against the comps, a seller who needs a quick contract). A different stance needs `pricing.stance_reason`: judgment, figure-free, about this home, never a restatement of the market numbers (the script states those beside it). Leaving `pricing.stance` out takes the suggestion.

The price: the stance's point in the range, snapped to a portal search bracket. Buyers filter in steps, so $469,900 shows up in every search that stops at $470,000 and $475,000 drops out of "up to $470,000": the script takes the nearest $5,000 step less $100 under $1M ($469,900), the nearest $10,000 step less $1,000 above ($1,249,000), and the next step inward when that falls outside the range. The first two to three weeks bring the most showings: a price buyers see as fair turns them into offers, an ambitious one turns into a later cut from a weaker position.

**The agent's own price:** only when the agent chose it, `price_override` `{list_price, reason}` (`reason` judgment, figure-free). The report shows it as the agent's, beside the stance's price, and the other options are built around it; compute.py warns when it sits outside the range (`list_outside_range`).

## The Pricing Options

The script builds them from the recommended price, one step apart (a step is $5,000 or 2% of the price, whichever is larger, snapped to a search bracket):

1. **Top of the range:** a step above, never past the range's high end. Longer to contract, a likely price cut, and an expected sale at the recommended option's anyway.
2. **Recommended:** the stance's price.
3. **Competing-offer price:** a step below, never under the range's low end. Fast, and only if competing offers actually show up: when it nets more than the recommended option, the notes say its net depends on them.

Two options that land within 1% of each other aren't distinct strategies (the seller would compare nets that differ only by holding costs), so the script keeps one: Draw Offers usually leaves no room for a competing-offer price below it, and Premium none for a top-of-range price above it. Each option's time to contract comes from the market's recent median days on market (slower for the top of the range, faster for competing offers), and its seller credit from how often recent sellers paid buyer costs times the typical amount; the notes say so once. Give an option the agent's own time, seller credit or wording in `pricing.options.<top|recommended|competing|stay>` (`time` as a range with its unit, "3–6 weeks", or `months_to_contract`; `seller_credit` a number; `note` judgment), and `expected_sale` or `closing_date` only when the agent gave them. compute.py turns each time into holding costs (loan interest, HOA, insurance, utilities) and shows the net after holding, so a slower, higher price is compared fairly. If one option comes out ahead only because of an agent's figure (a smaller credit), compute.py warns (`top_nets_more`); say that in `pricing.note`.

**A reprice** (the agent's own listing that hasn't sold, `reprice` in report.json): the options are **Stay at Current Price** first, then price cuts only, each at least 1% under the current price whatever the stance: the recommended new price and the competing-offer price. No top-of-range option. `reprice.allow_increase` (only when the agent asked to price it higher) lifts that cap; when the stance's price then meets the current price, staying is the recommendation. When the current price leaves no cut inside the range, compute.py stops: ask the agent for their price (`price_override`). The market has already answered the current price, so Stay is slower, and its expected sale follows one rule, so two runs of the same listing agree: the current price times the recent sale-to-original-list ratio of sales that sat at least as long as this listing has (the days-on-market adjustment; all recent sales when fewer than 3 did), or the median adjusted value if that's lower. The ratio is already net of seller-paid costs, so Stay's own seller credit is added back for the net sheet to take off. compute.py derives it from the export as `reprice.stay_expected_sale` and uses it; an agent's own figure goes in `pricing.options.stay.expected_sale` (compute.py warns `stay_expected_high` when it's above the rule's). When Stay nets more than the recommended cut, the notes say the cut buys time and certainty, not a higher net. Without an export, give Stay's `expected_sale` by the same rule from the sales you were given. Name the listing's price history in the recommendation (the script writes it: compute.py's `price_history`), and say in words what the lower price changes (the search brackets it enters, the competition it now beats).

**A cut inside the same search bracket** changes no buyer's search: $474,900 to $464,900 stays in the $450,000 to $475,000 bracket, so the buyers who skipped the listing still don't see it as new. When the cut stays inside a bracket, its case rests on the portals' price-drop alerts, the competition it now beats and the days on market, never on new searches, and `pricing.note` says so. When the current price is still inside the supported range, the case for a cut rests on the days on market, the feedback and the market since it listed, not on the comps alone: say that plainly.

**A relist** (the home's own earlier listing expired, was canceled or withdrawn within the last 12 months, whoever had it, or undated, or a live listing the agent chose to treat as failed with `--listed-as failed`; a listing that ended longer ago is named as history and sets no cap; `relist` in report.json, from stats.py's `relist` or the property report's history): the market already turned that price down, and buyers see the history. No option lists above the failed price unless the agent gives a reason (a documented change since, such as a renovation or a market that has risen since a listing that ended long ago), written in `relist.reason_above`. The script caps every option, the stance's price included, at the search-bracket step just under the failed price (or at the failed price when that step falls below the range), and drops the top-of-range option when the cap leaves it within 1% of the recommended price. A failed price under the range stops compute.py: the agent decides (a reason to go higher, or their own price). Say why in `pricing.note` in one sentence, figure-free ("the market already tested that price, so no option starts above it"). compute.py finds a failed listing in the export when report.json has no `relist`. The script names the failed price and its days on market (`price_history`), without dates when the export has none; ask for the dates in the reply.

Each option's expected sale is compute.py's, by one rule, so two runs agree: its list price times stats.py's recent sale-to-final-list ratio (net of seller-paid costs; against the last asking price, so the cuts overpriced homes took don't count against this one), plus its own seller credit, to the nearest $500. Without final list prices in the export (a sold row whose current price is its sale price) or without an export, it's `market.sale_to_list` from the sales you were given, else 97% assumed, stated in `assumptions`. It's never below the bottom of the range (the comps are already net of seller credits, so the range is the home's value), never above the list price (except the competing-offer option) or the top of the range, an option above the recommended price expects the recommended option's sale (a higher price costs time and holding costs, not price), and a lower price never expects more than a higher one. An agent's own figure (`pricing.options.<role>.expected_sale`) is marked as ours, and compute.py warns `expected_sale_order` or `top_nets_more` on it. The table shouldn't suggest precision it doesn't have.

## The Scatterplot

Pick 1–3 callouts, usually the top comp and the strongest active competitor, each a home the chart plots (a sale or an active listing; never a pending one, and render.py names any callout that isn't on the chart). Each label is the full street address, street type included ("1471 Sedgefield Ct (For Sale)"). Labels move off markers and other labels on their own (the side you give is tried first); if render.py says labels still crowd, drop a callout or shorten its label. The subject is plotted at the recommended list price. The script marks the comp cards' sales as Comparable Sales (matched to the export by street address, ignoring letter case: "1512 Buttonbush Dr" matches "1512 BUTTONBUSH DR", but "Drive" doesn't match "DR", so keep the export's words and abbreviations), draws the other sales and the listings, the size-only trend line, and notes how many homes were left off the chart for size.
